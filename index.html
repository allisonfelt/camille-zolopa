About

“I am interested in mental health as public health: understanding distress and wellbeing across diverse populations, and working to close the health disparities faced by people living under structural oppression.”

Camille Zolopa is a PhD candidate in Counselling Psychology in the Faculty of Education at McGill University, supervised by Dr Dennis Wendt. Her doctoral comprehensive examination, passed with distinction, was a scoping review of harm reduction practices and possibilities among Indigenous populations in Australia, Canada, and the United States — a line of research she continues to pursue.

Her path into psychology began in anthropology, which taught her to notice both the pervasiveness and the specificity of cultural influence, and left her with a lasting affinity for qualitative methods. What drew her onward was the wish to pair the study of culture with an action orientation: to examine how a person’s cultures shape their thoughts, feelings, and health behaviors, and to use that knowledge to build interventions actually suited to them. Most psychological knowledge, she notes, has been generated from a narrow slice of the world’s population; cultural-clinical psychology widens the lens.

Before returning to McGill she completed a Master of Science in Clinical Research Methodology at Fordham University and a Bachelor of Arts (Honours) in Psychology with Great Distinction at Concordia University, alongside an earlier Bachelor of Arts in Anthropology from McGill. Her work spans supervised consumption sites, opioid policy, youth mental health during COVID-19, hepatitis C care among people who inject drugs, and response-style bias in cross-cultural assessment — consistently asking questions that reflect intersectional identities, and consistently done in collaboration with community stakeholders and knowledge users.

Alongside her research, Camille is completing clinical training in individual psychotherapy through pre-doctoral practica in Montréal, and volunteers with Projets Autochtones du Québec, a shelter serving Indigenous clients in downtown Montréal.

She works in English and reads and speaks basic French and Spanish, and holds both American and Canadian citizenship.

Research

Her dissertation investigates drug use harm reduction and service use among Indigenous people living in Montréal — work grounded in holistic and relational perspectives, and in the cultural and psychosocial strengths that people bring to their own care.

More broadly, Camille’s research centers on harm reduction and substance use, with a particular focus on the wellbeing of Indigenous populations and other underserved communities. Recent and ongoing work includes:

  • Harm reduction practices and possibilities among Indigenous populations in Australia, Canada, and the United States
  • Supervised consumption and injection site use, overdose intervention, and Canada’s safer opioid supply research agenda
  • Youth mental health, wellbeing, and substance use during the COVID-19 pandemic
  • Sex and gender differences in hepatitis C virus risk and care among people who inject drugs
  • Extreme response style and cross-cultural bias in psychological assessment among Latinx populations
  • Aging among people who use illicit opioids, cocaine, or methamphetamine
